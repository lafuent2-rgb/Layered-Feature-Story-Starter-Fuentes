Building Shade in the Desert

Phoenix is responding to extreme heat with trees, built shade, cooling centers, and neighborhood projects designed to make outdoor spaces safer and more comfortable.

L2-L3 · Why Shade Matters in Phoenix

Extreme heat is one of the most serious environmental challenges facing Phoenix. Shade can make outdoor spaces more usable by reducing direct exposure to the sun, especially in places where people walk, wait for transportation, work outdoors, or spend time in public spaces. Because of this, Phoenix has begun treating shade as an important part of the city's public infrastructure rather than only as a landscaping feature.

The City of Phoenix created the Shade Phoenix Plan to expand and maintain both trees and built shade throughout the city. According to the city, tree canopy currently varies greatly between neighborhoods. At the census tract level, tree canopy ranges from about 2 percent to 30 percent, while the median tract has about 11 percent tree canopy. These differences mean that some neighborhoods have much less protection from direct sunlight than others.

Shade is becoming an important part of how Phoenix prepares public spaces for extreme heat.

The Shade Phoenix Plan includes 36 actions organized around expanding shade, maintaining existing shade, evaluating progress, and educating the public. Over five years, the plan is expected to support more than 27,000 new trees and 550 new shade structures. Phoenix also states that more than half of the total investment will be directed toward low to moderate income communities, where heat vulnerability and access to shade can be especially important.

L4-L5 · Heat Relief Beyond Trees

Trees and shade structures are only one part of Phoenix's approach to extreme heat. The city also operates cooling centers, hydration stations, and respite centers where people can escape dangerous temperatures and access water. These locations are free, open to the public, and do not require identification. They provide an immediate form of heat relief while longer term projects such as tree planting and shade construction continue to develop across the city.

A Broader Heat Response

Phoenix's 2026 Heat Response Plan includes 23 actions organized into eight strategies. These actions include providing publicly accessible cool spaces and drinking water, preparing first responders for heat emergencies, expanding outreach, and connecting residents with additional services. The city also plans to continue extended-hour cooling centers and a 24-hour respite center during the summer.

This combination of short term relief and long term shade planning shows how Phoenix is approaching extreme heat from more than one direction. Cooling centers can provide immediate protection during dangerous temperatures, while trees and permanent shade structures can gradually make streets, parks, sidewalks, and other public spaces more comfortable. Together, these efforts are intended to make heat relief easier to access across the city.

Building for the Future

Where shade is placed can matter just as much as how much shade is added. Areas around sidewalks, bus stops, parks, schools, and other public spaces are places where residents may spend time outside with limited protection from the sun. Phoenix's planning efforts give the city a way to identify areas where additional trees or built shade could have a greater effect. The goal is not only to increase the total amount of shade, but also to improve access to it in places where people regularly walk, wait, gather, and travel through the city.

Phoenix's challenge is not simply adding more shade, but deciding where shade can make the greatest difference and maintaining it over time. The Shade Phoenix Plan includes strategies for expanding shade, preserving existing trees and structures, measuring progress, and educating residents about heat and shade. By combining those long-term investments with seasonal heat relief services, the city is building a broader system for helping residents live with extreme desert temperatures.

Extreme heat will remain part of life in Phoenix, so these projects are designed as long term investments rather than temporary fixes. Trees take time to mature, shade structures require maintenance, and cooling services must be prepared before the hottest months arrive. By combining these approaches, Phoenix is trying to create public spaces that remain usable during increasingly difficult summer conditions while also giving residents more options for immediate relief when temperatures become dangerous.
